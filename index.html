Jutlandian Reserve
- The Tale of Morden-day Urbanisation

Copenhagen has changed a lot in recent years: the streets are cleaner, the coffee is better, and the prices are much, much higher. For locals mourning the loss of “old Copenhagen” and looking for someone to blame, newcomers from Jutland are an easy target.

The long-running “feud” between born-and-bred Copenhageners and newcomers from Jutland shows no sign of cooling off. The newcomers are easily distinguishable by their accents, but who exactly are the migrants from Jutland, and where do they settle in the city? What patterns can we observe, and what do they reveal about this growing group?

Scope note: map and resident counts use 1977-2026. Joint education + origin analysis uses overlapping years 1985-2024.

Development Over Time by Copenhagen Area

When we map the density of Jutlanders across Copenhagen's neighbourhoods over time, a familiar pattern emerges: they have excellent timing.

The timeline stretches back to the mid-1970s, when the numbers were already high. Drawn in by jobs and education, people from rural Denmark arrived in the capital and settled where housing was cheap and plentiful. The data shows the highest concentrations in Nørrebro and Østerbro — industrial, working-class districts that were a bit rough around the edges, but affordable and, crucially, available.

Then came the late 1980s and early 1990s, and something curious happened: they left. Or at least, many of them did. Across neighborhoods, the number of Jutlanders declines steadily from the 1980s through the mid-90s and into the early 2000s.

As industry faded, jobs disappeared, and these areas struggled with unemployment and social challenges, the charm disappeared and so did the Jutlanders.

But the story doesn’t end there. From around 2000 to 2010, the curve shoots up again. The biggest surge appears in the very neighborhoods once written off, Nørrebro, Østerbro, Amager, and especially Vesterbro. By the 2010s, these areas had been “sanitized”, renovated, and carefully rebranded. Cafés replaced factories, rents climbed accordingly, and suddenly neighborhoods like Nørrebro was no longer a compromise, it was a lifestyle. And, right on cue, the Jutlanders returned.

A City-Wide Picture: Who Is Actually Moving In?

Zoom out and the story gets interesting. Since 1977, the share of Jutland-born residents across Copenhagen has stayed remarkably stable, hovering around 15%. It dipped in the 90s and came back. Meanwhile, the share of people actually born in Copenhagen has fallen consistently from around 55% to 46%. So the city is not being "taken over" by Jutlanders. It is becoming more diverse in origin and Jutlanders are just one visible piece of that shift.

And not all neighbourhoods are equally attractive. In 2024, Vesterbro-Kongens Enghave leads the ranking with 17.4% Jutland-born, followed closely by Indre By and Nørrebro. Brønshøj-Husum brings up the rear at 10.7%. Make of that what you will.

The Districts That Changed the Most

Not all districts transformed equally. The districts that attracted the most Jutlanders are also the ones where education levels rose the fastest: Vesterbro, Nørrebro, and Indre By lead on both dimensions. These were once the neighbourhoods nobody was fighting over. Somehow, the Jutlanders ended up in exactly the right place at exactly the right time.

Early Movers or Followers? The Timing Question

The animated scatter shows the two trends move together. But "which came first?" Did Jutlanders move into already-educated, gentrified neighbourhoods? Or did they arrive early, before the transformation happened?

Jutlanders moved in when these areas were still cheap and rough, and stayed through the entire transformation. The café followed them, not the other way around.

So, Are the Jutlanders Really to Blame?

Copenhagen's neighbourhoods have been quietly transformed over the past 40 years. The Jutlanders who arrived in the 1970s and 80s did not find polished, expensive districts. They found working-class areas that were, frankly, a bit rough. But they stayed. And as the city changed around them, so did the profile of who was moving in.

By 2024, the districts with the highest concentration of Jutlanders are also the most educated in the city. Whether Jutlanders drove that transformation, or simply had excellent timing, is hard to say. But the data is clear on one thing: the share of Jutland-born residents has been stable for decades, while the share of Copenhagen-born residents has fallen considerably. If someone is "taking over", it is not the Jutlanders.

The stereotype doesn't survive contact with the data. The accent might stay, but the narrative needs updating.

What This Analysis Cannot Tell You

We are honest about what the data can and cannot show. Correlation is not causation — we do not claim that Jutlanders caused gentrification, or that rising education caused more Jutlanders to arrive. We show that these district-level trends move together over decades. What drives what requires better data than we have.

  • The education dataset covers Danish-origin residents as a whole — we cannot see whether individual Jutlanders are more or less educated than Copenhagen-born residents. That would require individual-level microdata from Statistics Denmark.
  • The resident register extends to 2026, but the education data ends in 2024. The joint analysis uses the overlapping period 1985–2024.
  • All results are at district level. We cannot track individual migration pathways or life outcomes.
