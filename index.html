Jutlandian Reserve
- The Tale of Morden-day Urbanisation

Copenhagen has changed a lot in recent years: the streets are cleaner, the coffee is better, and the prices are much, much higher. For locals mourning the loss of "old Copenhagen" and looking for someone to blame, newcomers from Jutland are an easy target. The long-running "feud" between born-and-bred Copenhageners and newcomers from Jutland shows no sign of cooling off. The newcomers are easily distinguishable by their accents, but who exactly are the migrants from Jutland, and where do they settle in the city? What patterns can we observe, and what do they reveal about this growing group?

Scope note: map and resident counts use 1977-2026. Joint education + origin analysis uses overlapping years 1985-2024.

Key Findings

The stereotype is loud, but the data is more nuanced. Jutland-born share in the district panel is fairly stable at city level, while the local-born share falls much more over time.

How Districts Changed Over Time

In several districts, Jutland-born counts dip during late-industrial decline and rise again in periods associated with urban redevelopment. This supports a timing story, not a "takeover" story.

Timeline years: 1977-2026 (resident register only).

Evidence on Education and Gentrification

When we join district origin composition with district education composition (1985-2024), we see a consistent positive association: districts with higher high-education shares also tend to have higher Jutland-born shares.

Interpretation and Limits

The story supports correlation, not causation. We do not claim that rising education causes Jutland migration or vice versa. We show that these district-level trends co-move over decades.

  • Education dataset in this export is limited to Danish-origin aggregation, not individual origin-level education by subgroup.
  • Some resident-only views extend to 2026, while merged analysis ends in 2024 due to data overlap.
  • Results are district-level and cannot identify individual mobility pathways.
